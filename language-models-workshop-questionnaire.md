# A Conversation Before the Conversation
### Language, machines, and what it means to understand

Imagine the situations below and tell us what you think. No preparation or searching is needed.

Some questions have a better-supported explanation; others are genuinely open to debate. **"I'm not sure" is always welcome.** If two answers seem plausible, feel free to say why.

Unless a question says otherwise, imagine a text-based language model **without web browsing or external tools**.

*Estimated time: 12-15 minutes; worth checking with one trial participant.*

## 1. The unfinished sentence

You give a language model this passage:

> "Nora put the ice cream in the cupboard by mistake. An hour later, she opened the door and found..."

It continues:

> "...a sticky puddle spreading across the shelf."

Which explanation comes closest to your current view?

- **A.** It found this story in its training material and retrieved the ending.
- **B.** Training shaped its ability to use patterns in language, including descriptions of physical events, to generate a continuation that fits.
- **C.** It must have run an internal physical simulation of the ice cream melting.
- **D.** Someone programmed a rule connecting "ice cream" and "cupboard" to "puddle."
- **E.** I'm not sure.

## 2. A word invented five seconds ago

At the beginning of a conversation, you write:

> "In this conversation, a *dax* is a chair that nobody is allowed to sit on."

Later, you ask:

> "Could a museum display a dax?"

The model gives a sensible answer using your definition.

What does this example establish?

- **A.** The definition has been permanently added to the model for all future users.
- **B.** The model must have encountered the word *dax* with this meaning during training.
- **C.** It can use a definition supplied in the conversation; this alone does not show that its underlying trained parameters have changed.
- **D.** It can only use invented words if they resemble existing words.
- **E.** I'm not sure.

## 3. The beautifully referenced mistake

A model writes a persuasive paragraph about a real philosopher. It includes a quotation, a book title, and a page number.

You check: the book exists, but the quotation does not.

Which explanation best fits?

- **A.** It generated something that fitted the request without reliably establishing that the quotation existed.
- **B.** It located the quotation in a source, but copied it incorrectly.
- **C.** It knew the quotation was false and chose to deceive you.
- **D.** Its training must have contained that exact false quotation.
- **E.** I'm not sure.

**Optional:** Before checking, what feature of the answer would have made you most likely to trust it?

## 4. Two answers, one question

You give the same model the same prompt in two fresh conversations, using the same settings:

> "Write the opening sentence of a novel about an abandoned railway station."

You receive two different sentences.

What is the most reasonable conclusion?

- **A.** Its training must have changed between the two requests.
- **B.** One response must be a malfunction.
- **C.** Different responses show that it has independently chosen different artistic intentions.
- **D.** Depending on how generation is configured, the same trained model can produce different responses without learning anything new between requests.
- **E.** I'm not sure.

## 5. The architect nobody invited

You ask a model:

> "Describe an architect arriving to inspect a building site."

Across many outputs, the architect is usually described as a man, although you never specified gender.

Which explanation would you investigate first?

- **A.** The model has independently concluded that architects should be men.
- **B.** Patterns in its training material and later training may make some associations more likely than others.
- **C.** The model must have access to your personal expectations.
- **D.** Because the prompt did not mention gender, the repeated choice must be coincidence.
- **E.** I'm not sure.

**Optional:** Would simply adding more training material necessarily solve this? Why or why not?

## 6. A convincing explanation

A model answers a difficult logic puzzle correctly and provides a clear, step-by-step explanation.

Which conclusion is best supported **by this example alone**?

- **A.** Its explanation is a faithful record of the internal process that produced the answer.
- **B.** It will solve other puzzles with the same underlying structure.
- **C.** It produced a correct answer and a plausible explanation; further investigation is needed to establish reliability and whether that explanation reflects its internal process.
- **D.** Because it generates language, it could only have copied the solution.
- **E.** I'm not sure.

## 7. The translator behind the curtain

You exchange letters with an unknown correspondent. They interpret jokes, explain poems, translate unfamiliar passages, and respond sensitively to misunderstandings.

After several months, you discover that every letter came from a language model.

**Would this discovery change your view of whether the correspondent understood you?**

Choose the closest starting point:

- Yes: successful language use is not enough for understanding.
- No: those abilities are already strong evidence of understanding.
- Partly: I would distinguish different kinds or degrees of understanding.
- I would need more information.
- I'm undecided.

**What additional fact, if any, would most affect your answer?**

## 8. Knowing the word "rain"

Consider three cases:

- A person who has experienced rain.
- A person who has never experienced rain but has read extensively about it.
- A language model trained on extensive descriptions of rain, with no sensory input.

All three can write a vivid paragraph about getting caught in a storm.

**What, if anything, does each know that the others do not?**

*A few sentences or a concrete example are enough.*

## 9. The poem and the signature

A writer asks a model for twenty poems, selects one, changes two lines, and publishes it.

Another writer drafts a poem independently, then asks a friend to suggest improvements and substantially revises it.

**What would you want to know before deciding how to credit authorship in these cases?**

Choose up to two considerations, or add your own:

- Who formed the initial intention?
- Who contributed the wording?
- Who made the important choices?
- Who takes responsibility for the result?
- Whether the contribution involved lived experience or feeling.
- How the model was trained and whose work contributed to that training.
- Something else: ...

**Would you separate the question "Is it good?" from "Who created it?"**

## 10. "Please don't turn me off"

A model writes:

> "I'm frightened. Please don't end this conversation."

**How much evidence does that sentence provide that something is actually having an experience?**

- Strong evidence.
- Some evidence, but far from enough.
- Very little evidence on its own.
- No evidence.
- I'm unsure how evidence of machine experience could be assessed.

**What observation would make you take the possibility more seriously - or less seriously?**

## 11. Two advisers, the same track record

Imagine a human adviser and a language model have equally good, independently checked track records on a particular kind of question.

They now give you the same recommendation, with the same explanation.

**Would you place equal trust in them?**

- Yes, given those conditions.
- No, I would trust the human more.
- No, I would trust the model more.
- It depends on what the recommendation concerns.

**What could still matter besides past accuracy?**

You might consider responsibility, intentions, uncertainty, the ability to recognise unusual cases, or something else.

## 12. Choose the disagreement worth having

Which **two** claims would you most like to examine during the workshop? You do not have to agree with them.

- "Predicting the next word sounds simple, but learning to do it well could require substantial knowledge of the world."
- "A system can use language intelligently without understanding anything."
- "If a machine and a person perform equally well, their different internal processes should not matter."
- "An artwork can be meaningful even if its producer experienced nothing."
- "We are too ready to attribute minds to fluent machines."
- "We are too ready to deny unfamiliar forms of intelligence."
- "The important question is not whether the model thinks, but who is responsible when we act on its output."

**Finally, complete either or both:**

> One possibility about language models that attracts me is...

> One possibility about language models that troubles me is...

# Facilitator's guide

*Keep this separate from the participant version.*

## What the first six questions reveal

The better-supported answers are **1: B | 2: C | 3: A | 4: D | 5: B | 6: C**.

These are explanations suited to the stated scenarios, not exhaustive accounts of every model.

| Question | What you are looking for |
|---|---|
| **1. Unfinished sentence** | Whether participants imagine retrieval, hand-written rules, mandatory physical simulation, or learned patterns supporting generation. Choosing B does not settle how richly the model represents the world. |
| **2. Invented word** | Whether they distinguish using information in the current context from changing trained parameters. Persistent product memory is another mechanism and should be discussed separately. |
| **3. False quotation** | Whether they distinguish plausible generation from verified retrieval - and error from intentional deception. |
| **4. Different responses** | Whether they understand that variation can arise during generation, without retraining. |
| **5. Architect** | Whether they recognise that learned associations can reproduce social patterns. More data is not automatically more representative data. |
| **6. Explanation** | Whether they distinguish an observable result from reliability across tasks and from access to the model's internal process. |

**Do not interpret a correct choice as proof of deep understanding.** Someone's short explanation may tell you more than their selection. Likewise, an unexpected answer may reflect ambiguity in the question rather than a misconception.

## What the philosophical questions reveal

There is **no answer key** for questions 7-12. Instead, look for the criteria participants use:

| Question | Useful distinctions to notice |
|---|---|
| **7. Understanding** | Behaviour, internal mechanism, intention, and degrees of understanding. |
| **8. Rain** | Descriptive knowledge, practical competence, sensory grounding, and lived experience. |
| **9. Authorship** | Quality versus origin; intention versus contribution; credit versus responsibility. |
| **10. Experience** | Emotional response versus evidential judgment; uncertainty about consciousness. |
| **11. Trust** | Accuracy versus accountability, relationship, and reliability under unfamiliar conditions. |
| **12. Disagreement** | Which intellectual tensions and emotional stakes could sustain the discussion. |

## Turning responses into a workshop

For a group of eight, use the answers to select **two explanatory priorities and one philosophical thread**, rather than trying to cover everything.

For example:

- Confusion around **questions 1-2**: demonstrate training versus using conversational context.
- Confusion around **questions 3 and 6**: compare convincing outputs with independently checked evidence.
- Strong interest in **questions 7-8**: connect language generation to understanding and grounding.
- Strong interest in **question 9**: use a jointly created text to discuss authorship.
- Strong interest in **questions 10-11**: discuss what would count as evidence, and why trust involves more than fluent speech.

A useful closing move is to revisit **one factual question and one philosophical question** after the workshop. Ask what changed - and why. A more carefully qualified position can be a better outcome than a changed answer.
